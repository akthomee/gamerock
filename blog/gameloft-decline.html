What Happened to Gameloft?

There was a time when Gameloft was the undisputed king of mobile gaming. Today, they are a shadow of their former selves. Here is the story of how the giant fell, and why we still miss them.

7 Min Read

If you owned a smartphone between 2009 and 2014, you knew the name Gameloft. They were the absolute pinnacle of mobile gaming. When phones were still running on 3-inch screens and physical keypads, Gameloft was pumping out games that looked like console titles.

They gave us N.O.V.A., a first-person shooter that somehow felt like Halo in your pocket. They gave us Asphalt, the arcade racing series that still holds up today. They gave us Modern Combat, Dungeon Hunter, Gangstar, and Nova Legacy. For a long time, Gameloft was the reason to own an Android phone or an iPhone.

But today? Gameloft is a shell of its former self. Their Play Store page is filled with idle clickers, match-3 games, and endless Disney tie-ins. Their legendary premium games have been abandoned, stripped of their servers, and left to die. So, what happened? How did the king of mobile gaming fall so hard?

The Golden Era: Consoles in Our Pockets

To understand the fall, you have to remember the peak. In the early 2010s, mobile processors were getting faster, and Gameloft was right there to push them to the limit. They had a reputation for "borrowing" ideas from console games (N.O.V.A. was famously inspired by Halo, Modern Combat by Call of Duty, Gangstar by Grand Theft Auto). But nobody cared. The games were fun, polished, and mind-blowing for a mobile device.

You have to remember how we played them. Buying a Gameloft game meant spending $6.99 upfront. There were no ads. There were no energy timers. You paid your money, you downloaded the 800 MB OBB file, and you played a full, complete campaign. They treated mobile games with the same respect as console games, and the audience rewarded them for it.

The Shift: The Free-to-Play Plague

Around 2014, the industry shifted. The "freemium" model exploded. Games like Candy Crush and Clash of Clans proved that you could make ten times more money by giving a game away for free and charging for in-app purchases (IAP) and microtransactions.

Gameloft panicked. Instead of sticking to their premium roots, they pivoted hard. They started taking their most beloved franchises and injecting them with energy timers, pay-to-win mechanics, and loot boxes. Asphalt 8 was a turning point; it was free, but you had to grind for hours or pay to unlock cars that used to be readily available.

The original N.O.V.A. 3 was a premium masterpiece. Then came N.O.V.A. Legacy. It was a stripped-down, mobile-first, online-only game full of microtransactions. It felt cheap. The soul was gone.

The Vivendi Takeover

Behind the scenes, things were getting worse. In 2016, a massive French media conglomerate called Vivendi launched a hostile takeover of Gameloft. Vivendi was known for telecoms and music, not game development.

They forced out the founding Guillemot brothers (who went on to run Ubisoft). With the original leadership gone, the new corporate overlords at Vivendi stripped the company down. They closed studios. They cancelled original projects. They looked at the balance sheets and realized that making cheap, licensed games (like Disney Magic Kingdoms or My Little Pony) was far more profitable and less risky than building a blockbuster premium shooter.

The Great Delisting

This is the part that hurts the most. As Android and iOS updated, older games broke. Instead of paying developers to update their old masterpieces to support 64-bit processors and modern Android, Gameloft just... delisted them.

That's why you can't download N.O.V.A. 2 or N.O.V.A. 3 from the Play Store anymore. They are gone. The DRM servers that verified your purchase were shut down. Multiplayer servers were shut down. Thousands of games were left as digital ghosts.

Gameloft essentially erased their own history. They decided that maintaining their legacy was a waste of money.
