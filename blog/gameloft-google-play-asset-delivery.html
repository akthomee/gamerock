Gameloft acquires 10% more new users with Google Play Asset Delivery

The old APK + OBB system is dead. Here is how Gameloft used Google's Play Asset Delivery (PAD) to cut CDN costs, fight pirates, and boost user retention across their biggest titles.

6 Min Read

If you’ve ever tried to download a large Android game from a third-party site, you know the pain of the APK + OBB system. You download a small APK file, then you have to hunt down an 800 MB OBB file, extract it, and place it in the exact right folder (like /Android/obb/com.gameloft...) or the game crashes on launch.

For years, Gameloft relied on this archaic system. It was a nightmare for users, but it was also a nightmare for Gameloft. They had to pay massive Content Delivery Network (CDN) bills to host those giant OBB files, and they had zero control over who was downloading them.

That changed when Gameloft adopted Google Play Asset Delivery (PAD). According to a recent case study shared by Google, migrating to PAD didn't just save them money—it actually increased their new user acquisition by 10%. Here is how they did it, and why it matters for the future of Android gaming.

What is Play Asset Delivery (PAD)?

Play Asset Delivery is Google’s modern replacement for OBB files. Instead of forcing the user to download a massive file all at once, PAD allows developers to break their game assets into smaller, dynamic chunks.

These chunks can be delivered in three ways:

  • Install-time: Downloaded at the same time as the base APK.
  • Fast-follow: Downloaded automatically in the background as soon as the player opens the game for the first time.
  • On-demand: Downloaded only when the player actually reaches a specific level or needs a specific asset (like a high-res texture pack).

The Problem with APK + OBB

Gameloft’s biggest titles—like Asphalt 8, Asphalt 9, and Minion Rush—were sitting on years of legacy code. They relied heavily on the OBB system.

When a user downloaded these games from the Play Store, Google would push the APK, but Gameloft had to host the OBB on their own servers. This caused three massive problems:

  1. High Drop-off Rates: If the OBB download failed, or if the user ran out of storage halfway through, the game would break. Users would instantly uninstall and leave a 1-star review.
  2. Piracy: It was incredibly easy for modding sites to steal the OBB, strip the DRM from the APK, and re-upload the whole game for free.
  3. Server Costs: Gameloft had to pay for the bandwidth every time someone downloaded an 800 MB OBB file.

How PAD Solved Gameloft’s Problems

By switching to Google Play Asset Delivery, Gameloft handed the heavy lifting over to Google’s infrastructure.

When a user installs Asphalt 9 today, Google handles the entire download process. If the user’s connection drops, Google Play automatically resumes the download without corrupting the files. Gameloft no longer has to pay for the CDN bandwidth, and because PAD files are stored securely on Google's servers, they are much harder to pirate.

But the most surprising metric was user retention. By eliminating the confusing OBB step, Gameloft saw a 10% increase in new users successfully completing the onboarding process. Players were actually getting into the game instead of bouncing off a "Download Failed" screen.

What This Means for Modders and GameRock

If you are a regular visitor to GameRock, you know we provide APK + OBB downloads for classic games. You might be wondering what happens to those games now that PAD is the standard.

For newer games (like Asphalt 9 or Disney Speedstorm), PAD makes modding and archiving much harder. Because the assets are delivered dynamically and tied to your Google account, you can't just extract a single OBB file and share it. This is Google's way of locking down the ecosystem and stopping piracy.

However, for the older, delisted classics (like N.O.V.A. 2 and N.O.V.A. 3), the old OBB system is actually a blessing. Because those games are no longer on the Play Store, they don't use PAD. They are frozen in time. That is why we can still preserve them, host the OBB files, and keep the legacy alive.

The Future of Mobile Gaming

Gameloft’s success with PAD is a sign of things to come. As mobile games get larger and more complex, the old APK + OBB system simply cannot survive. Google is pushing all developers to use App Bundles and Play Asset Delivery.

For developers, it means lower costs and higher retention. For players, it means a cleaner, more seamless installation process—no more hunting for folders or restarting downloads. And for the modding community? It means we have to adapt, and focus on preserving the games that came before the cloud took over.
